Everyday High-Protein Foods for GLP-1 Users

The hardest part of eating well on a GLP-1 medication is not knowing that protein matters. It is hitting a protein target on an appetite that has genuinely halved, on a day when the thought of chicken is unbearable.

This page is organised around that reality — not by food group, but by the situation you are actually in when you open the fridge. If you do not yet know your daily target, start with the protein calculator; if you want the reasoning behind the numbers, that is in the nutrition guide.

🎯 The practical target

Most adults losing weight on a GLP-1 are aiming for roughly 1.2–1.6 g of protein per kg of ideal body weight — commonly somewhere between 80 g and 130 g a day. Broken into four servings of 20–30 g, that is far less daunting than one number.

When nothing sounds good

This is the situation that derails protein intake more than any other, and it is usually worst in the days right after a dose or a titration step. Liquid and cold options work when solid food does not — they carry no cooking smell, need no preparation, and go down when a plate will not.

  • Ready-to-drink protein shakes — 20–30 g per bottle, no preparation, and the single most reliable fallback on a bad day
  • Greek yogurt — around 20 g per cup, cold and mild
  • Cottage cheese — around 14 g per half cup, slow-digesting
  • Protein powder in water or milk — 20–25 g, and you control the concentration if a full shake is too much volume
  • Bone broth — modest protein, but warm, salty and often tolerable when nothing else is

No-cook and minimal effort

Reduced appetite tends to reduce willingness to cook along with it. Keeping these on hand means a protein serving is never more than a minute away:

  • Protein bars — 15–20 g, shelf-stable, portable
  • Hard-boiled eggs — 6 g each, batch-cook a week at a time
  • Canned tuna or salmon — 20–25 g per tin, no cooking required
  • Jerky or meat sticks — 8–12 g per serving, no refrigeration
  • Rotisserie chicken — around 25 g per serving, and someone else did the cooking
  • String cheese — 6–8 g, useful for topping up a day that fell short

Shelf-stable staples worth keeping in the cupboard

The strategic move is to stock the non-perishable options *before* a bad stretch, because that is precisely when you will not feel like shopping.

  • Protein powder — the most protein per unit of volume and cost of anything on this page
  • Canned fish, chicken and beans
  • Red lentils and split peas — 18 g per cooked cup, and they cook down small
  • Protein pasta — roughly double the protein of regular pasta for the same volume
  • Nut butters — 7 g per two tablespoons, and calorie-dense when weight loss is running too fast
  • Powdered milk — stirs into soups, oats and sauces to raise protein invisibly

🥤 A note on shakes and bars

Whole food is generally the better default — it brings fibre and micronutrients a shake does not. But the best protein source is the one you will actually consume today. On a day when solid food is impossible, a shake is not a compromise; it is the thing standing between you and losing muscle.

Putting it together: hitting 100 g

Three ways the same target can be reached, depending on the kind of day you are having:

  • A good day: eggs at breakfast (12 g) · chicken at lunch (35 g) · Greek yogurt in the afternoon (20 g) · salmon at dinner (25 g) · string cheese (7 g) = 99 g
  • A low-effort day: ready-to-drink shake (30 g) · canned tuna (25 g) · protein bar (20 g) · cottage cheese (14 g) · hard-boiled eggs (12 g) = 101 g
  • A bad day: two ready-to-drink shakes (56 g) · Greek yogurt (20 g) · protein powder in milk (25 g) = 101 g, with almost no chewing involved

The point of the third row is that even a day when you cannot face food is a day you can hit your target. Plan for that day before it arrives.
